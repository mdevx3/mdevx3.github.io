01 Problem

Why is aggressive discounting destroying profitability?

A global B2B SaaS company was struggling to understand why revenue was growing while profitability was deteriorating. The dataset contained 9,994 transactions across multiple geographies, customer segments, and product lines — with discount rates ranging from 0% to 85%.

The core question was whether discounting was driving genuine growth or merely eroding margins. Answering it required going beyond summary statistics to rigorous statistical testing that could hold up to business scrutiny.

02 Process

Statistical testing to move beyond gut-feel analysis

  • Cleaned and standardised the dataset — corrected data types, fixed subregion misclassifications (e.g. Israel placed in EU-EAST), and confirmed zero missing values or duplicates across 9,994 rows.
  • Performed comprehensive EDA — distribution analysis, outlier detection, and segment-level profiling — to understand the dataset's shape before testing any hypothesis.
  • Ran a D'Agostino-Pearson normality test on both groups (discounted vs. non-discounted), confirmed non-normality in both, and selected a non-parametric approach accordingly.
  • Applied the Mann-Whitney U test to compare profit distributions between discounted and non-discounted transactions — yielding a medium-to-large effect size (rank-biserial ≈ 0.50).
  • Used Spearman rank correlation (ρ = −0.54, p ≈ 0) to quantify the monotonic inverse relationship between discount rate and profit.
  • Drilled down with heatmaps mapping total losses per product against both discount level and customer segment to surface where each product's leakage was concentrated.
  • Built an interactive Tableau dashboard visualising discount vs. profit by region and product, with drill-down filters for stakeholder exploration.
  • Synthesised findings into a stakeholder presentation with specific, actionable pricing and discount cap recommendations.

Every single one of the 1,871 loss-making transactions had a discount — and every one of the 4,798 non-discounted transactions was profitable. The margin stays healthy up to a 20% discount (+11.8%), but collapses at 30% (−10.1%) with the loss rate jumping from 13.7% to 91.6%. Discounts ≥ 30% alone account for ~$135K in losses — 88.7% of the company's total leakage.

The SMB segment bore the deepest losses (−$84,946), driven by its combination of high transaction volume and thin margins. "ContactMatcher" was the worst single product at −$70,923. A key exception: "FinanceHub" loses money even at low discounts (20–30%), pointing to a cost structure problem rather than excessive discounting — requiring re-pricing rather than a cap.

Recommendations: cap all discounts at 20% with managerial approval required above that threshold; shift SMB incentives away from price cuts toward bundling and extended trials; re-price FinanceHub at the base level; and implement automated margin alerts for any transaction projecting a loss before it closes.
